# Discussion — v7 (Genome Medicine)

## Two solutions to the same problem

Our data support a model in which two distinct metabolic strategies evolved
independently to sustain an energetically expensive brain under contrasting thermal
pressures — extending the thrifty genotype hypothesis to a bidirectional East-West
axis. The Eastern architecture — enhanced glycolysis (PFKM, LDHA), augmented
adipose storage (ADIPOQ), heightened cold thermosensation (TRPA1) — was fixed
over >30,000 years of Arctic selection, traceable to Eastern Neandertal lineage
(~110 ka) and early Siberian modern humans (~65 ka). The Western architecture —
lipogenesis (ACACA) and thermogenic regulation (PPARGC1A) assembled in two
independent Pleistocene and Neolithic layers — reflects adaptation to temperate
conditions and agricultural subsistence. Western T2D risk predates modernity:
Ötzi already harbored risk alleles and a metabolic phenotype optimized for
plant-based diets 5,300 years ago.

## From regulatory variant to glycolytic mechanism

The identification of a single Eastern-specific upstream variant at the LDHA/LDHC
locus (chr11:18,411,103 C>T) as a coordinated trans-eQTL suppressing LDHC
expression across seven metabolically relevant tissues simultaneously — skeletal
muscle (p = 3.71 × 10⁻⁶⁴, NES = −0.935), subcutaneous adipose
(p = 1.46 × 10⁻⁵⁹), visceral adipose (p = 1.11 × 10⁻⁴¹), whole blood
(p = 7.39 × 10⁻³⁰), pancreas (p = 7.33 × 10⁻²⁶), liver (p = 9.12 × 10⁻²⁴),
and brain cortex (p = 2.70 × 10⁻²²) — converts the East-West paleogenomic
dichotomy into a tractable molecular mechanism. Independent replication in the
eQTLGen whole-blood cohort (n = 31,684; Z = 75.96, p = 3.27 × 10⁻³¹⁰) at
flanking variants in strong LD confirms that this is among the most robust
cis/trans-eQTL signals in the human genome, not a statistical artefact of a
single dataset.

LDHC encodes the muscle/testis isoform of lactate dehydrogenase, catalysing the
final step of anaerobic glycolysis. Its coordinated downregulation across muscle,
adipose, pancreas, liver, and brain cortex by an Eastern-specific regulatory
variant of LDHA — the gene showing the highest Eastern variant count in our
dataset (42 variants) — establishes a direct mechanistic link between ancestral
Arctic-adapted glycolytic architecture and differential metabolic flux in tissues
central to T2D pathophysiology. The near-unit effect size (|NES| ≈ 1.0) in
skeletal muscle, liver, and brain cortex suggests that the Eastern regulatory
variant suppresses approximately half of the basal LDHC transcriptional output
in these tissues, a magnitude consistent with a major regulatory adaptation
rather than background variation. In calorically abundant environments, LDHC
suppression shifts the pyruvate/lactate equilibrium toward oxidative
phosphorylation — a thermogenic advantage under Arctic conditions that may impair
metabolic flexibility under conditions of caloric excess, contributing to the
earlier age of onset and more aggressive T2D presentation observed in AMR and
EAS populations.

The brain cortex eQTL signal (p = 2.70 × 10⁻²²) is particularly notable in light
of SLC2A3 invariance: while brain glucose *transport* was conserved across 430,000
years of hominin evolution, brain glucose *metabolism* retains Eastern-specific
regulatory variation. This dissociation — an invariant transporter alongside a
variable metabolic effector — suggests that the Eastern brain prioritised glucose
*acquisition* over *aerobic processing*, a distinction with potential implications
for neurocognitive aspects of T2D not captured by peripheral biomarkers.

## Regulatory ghost adaptation and incomplete purification

LDHA missense variants shared between D17 and Ust'-Ishim are absent from gnomAD
v4, indicating active purifying selection on coding changes. Yet the surrounding
regulatory landscape persists at 25–53% frequency in AMR/EAS populations. We term
this pattern *regulatory ghost adaptation*: coding changes eliminated by purifying
selection, regulatory architecture co-selected with them persisting in non-coding
space. Non-coding regulatory variants are purged slowly, particularly when their
phenotypic cost manifests only under calorically abundant conditions historically
novel in evolutionary terms. Ten thousand years since the Last Glacial Maximum and
500 years since contact with Western dietary patterns represent at most 400–500
human generations — insufficient to dismantle polygenic regulatory architectures
assembled over tens of thousands of years of Arctic selection.

The multi-tissue eQTL architecture of the LDHA/LDHC locus reinforces this
interpretation. The Eastern-specific upstream variant chr11:18,411,103 suppresses
LDHC expression with effect sizes of |NES| ≈ 1.0 across muscle, adipose, liver,
pancreas, and brain — the five organ systems that collectively define metabolic
syndrome. This is not a single-tissue phenomenon: it is a coordinated, whole-body
regulatory programme inherited from Pleistocene Arctic adaptation, now operating
in metabolic environments its architecture was never designed to encounter.

## The Beringia bottleneck: antifreeze in the veins

The peopling of the Americas exported this Pleistocene metabolic architecture
into environments it was not designed for. Unadmixed Native American populations
— Surui and Karitiana of the Amazon (100%), Pima of northern Mexico (77%), Maya
of Mesoamerica (67%) — carry the Eastern NCDN variants at frequencies
indistinguishable from East Asian populations (78%), confirming that the full
Eastern architecture crossed Beringia intact. The stepwise frequency reduction
observed from unadmixed Native Americans through populations of increasing
European admixture — Peruvians (53%), Mexicans (39%), AMR gnomAD (31%) —
to the near-absence in NFE Europeans (3%) constitutes a quantitative genomic
record of the demographic consequences of the 16th-century conquest. The
admixture signal, treated here as a confound, is itself clinically informative:
it predicts that individuals of predominantly indigenous ancestry carry a higher
burden of Eastern regulatory architecture than their self-reported or genetically
inferred continental ancestry would suggest, and that this burden is
systematically underestimated in studies using continental ancestry labels
calibrated on admixed reference panels.

The metabolic price of Beringia is still being paid — in diabetes clinics across
Mexico City, Lima, Phoenix, and Yucatán — by populations whose ancestors carried
Arctic-optimised glycolytic machinery into tropical environments 15,000 years ago,
and whose regulatory architecture has not had sufficient evolutionary time to
adapt to the caloric abundance of modernity. The 500 years since contact have
added European regulatory variants to this architecture without replacing it,
creating metabolic mosaics that standard ancestry-based risk models are not
equipped to characterise.

## The human singularity and its metabolic price

Homo sapiens uniquely combined extreme encephalization with cold-environment
colonisation, enabled by behavioural and technological innovations — hunting,
fire, shelter, social cooperation. Both Eastern and Western lineages paid a
metabolic price for this combination. As globalisation and admixture render
continental ancestry labels increasingly imprecise, the East-West axis retains
clinical utility because it is defined by specific regulatory alleles — not
self-reported ethnicity — that will remain informative even as individuals no
longer identify with any single continental group. SLC2A3 invariance across
430,000 years of hominin evolution establishes brain glucose priority as the
non-negotiable constraint around which both architectures were independently
organised. The periphery adapted; the brain never compromised.

## Implications for precision medicine

SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and thiazolidinediones were validated
predominantly in European-ancestry cohorts. The LDHC trans-eQTL signal — present
at high frequency in EAS and unadmixed Native American populations, attenuated
but persistent in admixed AMR populations — suggests that Eastern metabolic
architecture creates differential glycolytic flux in skeletal muscle and liver
that may alter the pharmacodynamic context of these agents. SGLT2 inhibitors
achieve glycaemic control partly through muscle glucose uptake reallocation;
differential LDHC suppression in Eastern-architecture carriers may modify the
metabolic response to renal glucose excretion. GLP-1 receptor agonists act
partly through pancreatic beta-cell potentiation; the LDHC eQTL signal in
pancreas (p = 7.33 × 10⁻²⁶) identifies this tissue as a potential locus of
differential drug response. PPARγ agonists may show differential efficacy due to
ADIPOQ polarisation. These hypotheses are testable with existing pharmacogenomic
datasets stratified by LDHA/LDHC regulatory haplotype rather than continental
ancestry — a stratification that would recover Eastern architecture signal in
admixed AMR populations currently misclassified by continental labels.

The BMI thresholds documented by Caleyachetty et al. are not arbitrary: they are
the epidemiological shadow of the evolutionary divergence documented here.
Ancestry-informed calibration of these thresholds, anchored to specific
regulatory haplotypes rather than self-reported ethnicity, represents the most
tractable near-term clinical application of these findings. Functional validation
via CRISPR editing and iPSC-derived adipocyte and myocyte differentiation is
required to establish causality and is warranted as a direct extension of the
present work.

## Limitations

This analysis is limited by available high-coverage archaic genomes; Chagyrskaya
8, Loschbour, and Stuttgart LBK are not included. The conservative C→T/G→A damage
filter may remove genuine variants; however, PMDtools-based filtering (threshold
≥3) gave comparable directional results. The Sima de los Huesos SLC2A3 pileup
(mean depth 1.18×, hg38) is suggestive rather than definitive given the inherent
coverage limitations of 430,000-year-old specimens. Pairwise yn00 reflects
lineage-wide divergence; branch-specific analyses are warranted. Native American
population sample sizes in HGDP are small (n = 6–36); replication in larger
indigenous cohorts with appropriate ethical frameworks is required before clinical
translation. The eQTL signals reported here are observational; CRISPR functional
validation is needed to establish causality.
